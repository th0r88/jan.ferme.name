A name on the web

A small explanation of third-level .name domains, and the people who helped save them.

What is a third-level .name domain?

.name is a top-level domain intended for personal names. Its third-level registration model lets people use an address in the form firstname.lastname.name, such as jan.ferme.name or neil.fraser.name.

Domain levels are counted from right to left:

name — top level
The ending that identifies the .name namespace.
ferme — second level
The family-name part in this example.
jan — third level
The given-name part, completing jan.ferme.name.

This shared-surname model allows different people to register their own names under the same surname, rather than one person having to own the entire lastname.name domain.

How Neil Fraser helped preserve it

A personal domain can become part of someone's identity: an address used for a website, correspondence, and links collected over years. Losing it means more than moving a few files.

In his 10 November 2026 post, “.name Grandfathered”, Neil Fraser reports that Verisign withdrew a plan to terminate 22,000 domain names and email redirects.

Neil fought the proposal with legal help, alongside a wider community that submitted formal reconsideration requests, filed letters of support, and made its case to ICANN in person. He credits the outcome to that collective effort, including people within ICANN who supported them.

The outcome: existing third-level .name domains and email redirects will continue to be supported while at least one ICANN-accredited registrar offers these services at retail. Verisign still plans to close new registrations.
